Parametric inference for the average hazard approach in time-to-event analysis with sparse risk sets


Zihan Qian, Lu Tian, Hajime Uno
[stat.ME]

The average hazard (AH) is a recently proposed model-free summary of an event-time distribution. Over a prespecified window $[0,\tau]$, it equals the cumulative incidence probability at $\tau$ divided by the restricted mean survival time at $\tau$ and can be interpreted as a person-time incidence rate that does not depend on the censoring distribution. The difference and ratio of AHs from two groups, DAH and RAH, are between-group contrasts on absolute and relative scales without requiring proportional hazards. Existing nonparametric inference for AH and its contrasts is based on the Kaplan–Meier estimator of the survival function, and can become unreliable when risk sets become sparse or exhausted near $\tau$, as may occur in stratified analyses. As a practical remedy, we investigate parametric AH inference using Weibull and generalized gamma models. We use maximum-likelihood estimators and corresponding confidence intervals for group-specific AHs, DAH and RAH in both unstratified and stratified analyses. Simulations show that under correct model specification, little bias and near-nominal coverage can be achieved in settings where nonparametric inference is inadequately supported by observed data. We illustrate the method using a randomized trial comparing neratinib plus capecitabine with lapatinib plus capecitabine in metastatic breast cancer. Parametric inference provides a practical complement to nonparametric AH analysis when sparse or exhausted risk sets make inference at clinically relevant truncation times less reliable.
